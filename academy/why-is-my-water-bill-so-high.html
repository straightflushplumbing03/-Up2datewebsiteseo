Water leak symptoms

Why is my water bill so high all of a sudden?

A water bill that jumps without an obvious explanation is one of the clearest early signs of a hidden leak — and one of the easiest to dismiss as a fluke.

Schedule Diagnosis

The short answer

A sudden jump in a water bill usually comes from one of four things: a leak (most often a toilet flapper, a dripping fixture, an irrigation line, or a hidden pipe), more watering or more people at home, a longer billing period, or — with a budget-based rate — usage that crossed into a higher-priced tier. A five-minute meter check tells you whether water is moving when nothing should be running. If it is, and the toilets and irrigation check out, a hidden leak is the likely cause.

Start with the water meter

Turn off every fixture, appliance and irrigation zone, note the reading on your water meter, and check it again later without using any water. If it moved, water is leaving the system somewhere even though nothing should be running — a strong indicator of a leak. The U.S. EPA’s WaterSense program suggests a two-hour no-use window for this test. Our water meter test guide walks through it step by step.

Rule out the common leaks first

According to the EPA, the most common household leaks are worn toilet flappers, dripping faucets and other leaking valves. To check a toilet, put a few drops of food coloring in the tank; if color shows up in the bowl after about 10 minutes without flushing, the flapper is leaking (flush afterward so the tank doesn’t stain). A faucet dripping once per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons a year, and outdoors, check for a dripping spigot, a broken sprinkler head or an irrigation zone running longer than intended.

Check whether the bill is really about more water

Not every high bill is a leak. Before assuming the worst, compare the number of days in the billing period, any change in how many people are living in the home, and the season — irrigation demand rises in hot, dry months. Most water utilities show several months of usage online. A sudden, sustained jump that doesn’t match any of these is more consistent with an ongoing leak than a one-time spike.

Why a small change can cost more in South Orange County

Moulton Niguel Water District, which serves Laguna Niguel, Aliso Viejo and parts of neighboring cities, uses a budget-based rate structure. Each month a household gets an indoor water budget (based on household size and days billed) and an outdoor budget (based on irrigable area and local weather), and usage above that budget is billed at higher rates. That means a leak that adds water on top of normal use can push part of the bill into a more expensive tier. Other South OC districts set rates differently, so check your own bill.

Moulton Niguel customers can also see hourly usage and sign up for leak notifications in the district’s MyWater MNWD portal and app. Steady use in the middle of the night, when the household is asleep, is a classic sign of a leak. If you find and fix a leak, ask your water district whether it offers a bill adjustment — policies vary and aren’t guaranteed.

When it points to a slab or hidden leak

If your meter keeps moving with everything off and the toilets, faucets and irrigation check out, the leak is probably somewhere you can’t see: in a wall, under the slab, or in the line between the meter and the house. Other signs that point the same way include a warm spot on the floor or the sound of running water in a wall. The next step is professional leak detection to find the exact source before anything is opened up. We locate the leak first with acoustic, electronic and thermal imaging tools, then explain your repair options.

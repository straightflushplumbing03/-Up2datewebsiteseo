Insurance basics

Does homeowners insurance cover slab leaks?

The honest answer: it depends entirely on your specific policy, the cause of the leak, and your insurer's terms. Here's the general landscape.

The general pattern

What often matters most

  • Whether the leak was sudden and accidental, versus gradual wear over time
  • Whether resulting damage (not just the pipe itself) is what's being claimed
  • Your specific policy's language around "hidden water damage"
  • Whether the pipe failure was linked to lack of maintenance

The short answer

Often partly. Under many standard homeowners policies, a slab leak claim breaks into three costs that are treated differently: fixing the broken pipe itself is usually not paid, while the cost to open the slab or floor to reach the pipe and the water damage the leak caused often are — but only when the leak counts as a sudden, accidental event under your policy rather than long-term seepage, wear or corrosion. Your own policy wording decides it, so treat this page as background and confirm with your insurer.

The three parts of a slab leak claim

  • The pipe repair. The cost to fix the failed pipe is typically not covered, because the pipe failing on its own is treated as wear or maintenance.
  • Getting to the pipe (“tear-out”). Breaking out concrete, flooring or drywall to reach the leak, and putting it back, is often covered when the leak itself is a covered loss.
  • Resulting damage. Water damage to floors, walls, cabinets and belongings is the part most policies are written around — again, if the cause is a covered peril.

If a pipe is leaking but hasn’t damaged anything yet, there may be little to claim beyond what your policy says about access costs. Rerouting or repiping instead of a spot repair is a repair decision, not usually a coverage category — ask your adjuster how your policy treats it.

Sudden vs. gradual: the line that decides most claims

Homeowners policies commonly exclude damage from water that leaks “continuously or repeatedly” over weeks, months or years, and some versions of that exclusion name a specific period, such as 14 days or more. Slab leaks are hidden, so homeowners and insurers can disagree about how long one was running. California courts have weighed in on how these exclusions are read, and consumer group United Policyholders notes the timing question can turn on when the leak began, not when it was found.

Two practical points follow. First, act quickly once you notice a sign — a warm spot on the floor, the sound of running water, or a water bill that jumped — because ignoring known signs can hurt a claim. Second, an accurate, documented diagnosis of where the leak is and what the line looks like is the evidence everyone works from.

Policy wording worth checking

  • Is there a “continuous or repeated seepage or leakage” exclusion, and does it name a number of days?
  • Does the policy pay to “tear out and replace” part of the building to reach a leaking system?
  • Is there a limit or sublimit for water damage, or a separate deductible?
  • How are foundation movement, settling and cracking treated? These are commonly excluded on their own.
  • Do you have, or can you add, an endorsement for service lines or water backup? These are optional on many policies.

What about the cost of leak detection?

When a customer files an insurance claim, insurance typically reimburses the leak detection cost. As with the rest of the claim, that depends on your policy and the cause of the leak, so confirm with your insurer.

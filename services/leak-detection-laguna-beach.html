Where leaks show up in Laguna Beach homes

Older supply piping. The median Laguna Beach home was built in 1964 (City-Stats, 2023 ACS data), so many homes are around 60 years old. Age alone doesn't mean a leak, but in an older system one pinhole is a reason to check the condition of the rest of the pipe.

Buried lines on hillside and canyon lots. On a sloped lot the service line from the meter and the irrigation lines can run a long way underground, and a leak there may show up only as a higher bill, a soggy patch downhill, or a spot that stays green in a dry month.

Under the slab and inside walls. Homes on concrete slabs can leak under the floor, with a warm spot or the sound of running water as the clue; in multi-level hillside homes a leak upstairs often first appears as a stain on the ceiling below. See how we find slab leaks.

Why a slow leak matters more on a slope

Laguna Beach knows what water does to a hillside. On June 1, 2005, a landslide in Bluebird Canyon destroyed or severely damaged about 20 homes and about 350 were evacuated; early findings pointed to winter rains, and one resident described hearing her pipes rupture as the ground moved (Insurance Journal). The same report notes a 1978 slide that cost 24 families their homes. A small plumbing leak does not mean a slope is failing, but water that keeps escaping into the ground is worth locating promptly. If you also see new cracks or ground movement, contact the City of Laguna Beach and a geotechnical professional; that is outside what a plumber can assess.

Check for a leak yourself first

Laguna Beach County Water District's advice: turn off and leave off every tap inside and outside the home, record the meter reading, and check again in two to three hours. Many meters have a small red triangle that spins with even a small leak (LBCWD FAQ). If the meter moved, water is leaving the system somewhere. District side or your side?

Most of Laguna Beach, plus Emerald Bay, gets its water from Laguna Beach County Water District; South Laguna is served by South Coast Water District. Your bill names your district. Water coming up from the street, the meter box or a main is the district's to look at. A leak on your side of the meter is generally the owner's to find and fix, and LBCWD's permanent water-use rules list leaving a leaking pipe or fixture unrepaired as a prohibited use, so it pays to find it early.

How we work

Find the leak first, then a straight answer on the fix

1 · Isolate

Which line is leaking?

Pressure testing shows whether the hot line, cold line or the service line from the meter is losing water, so we know where to look.

2 · Pinpoint

Listen, trace and confirm

Acoustic equipment picks up the sound of pressurized water escaping, electronic locating traces where the pipe runs, and thermal imaging cross-checks hot-water leaks. The spot is marked for the smallest possible opening.

3 · Decide

Repair, reroute, or repipe

A single leak in a sound line is usually a spot repair. Repeat leaks in aging pipe may make a reroute or repipe the smarter call. We explain the trade-offs after diagnosis, never before.
